    Case study · Concept · 2026

    How I designed KOMA, a clip library for anime editors.

    KOMA is a concept I designed and built. It's a place where editors can find anime scenes, check them frame by frame and test a rough edit before downloading anything. Here's how it came together.

    01 · The problem

    Finding the clip takes longer than the edit.

    If you've ever made an anime edit, you know the routine. You remember a scene, then spend ages scrubbing through episodes or digging through clip sites for a clean copy. A lot of those sites are basically image galleries: a thumbnail, a title and a download button. You only find out the clip is 720p, watermarked or the wrong frame rate after you've downloaded it.

    So I wanted a library that tells you all of that up front, and lets you try a clip in an edit before you commit to it.

    02 · Who it's for

    Who it's for

    Short-form editors

    They're cutting to a trending sound and want it posted today. They need to scan fast and trust the preview.

    AMV editors

    Quality is everything. They check resolution, frame rate and whether a clip has been Twixtor'd before anything else.

    New editors

    Still learning the lingo. They need plain labels and tools that make sense without a tutorial.

    To be honest, these are my assumptions. I haven't tested them with real editors yet, so that's first on my list.

    03 · Concept

    Make it feel like editing software.

    Editors already live in Premiere, After Effects or CapCut, so I borrowed their language instead of making up my own. If you've used an editor before, most of KOMA should feel familiar.

    In editing softwareIn KOMA

    04 · Visual system

    Dark room, one pink.

    I wanted it to feel like editing at 2 a.m. The background is a deep purple-black, and there's only one bright color, a sakura pink. I save it for the important stuff: the playhead, the main buttons and whatever's selected. Every color has a light and a dark version, so the theme switch just works.     05 · All code

    There isn't a single image on this site.

    No images, no videos, no music files. Every thumbnail, preview and intro shot is drawn in code on a canvas, and the soundtrack is generated right in the browser. Each clip has a “seed”, a number that decides how its scene looks, so the same clip always comes out the same. Play with it below.

    06 · Key decisions

    Little decisions.

    • The intro only plays once. It's fun the first time and annoying the fifth. You can skip it, and you can replay it from the footer.
    • Sound is your choice. Browsers block audio until you click something anyway, so the intro just asks. That click is also what keeps the music in sync.
    • Previews only on hover. Only the clip under your mouse moves. 36 clips playing at once would be a mess, and slow.
    • Timecodes everywhere. Every preview shows where it is in the original episode, so you can find the moment again.
    • Reduced motion is respected. If your device asks for less motion, the intro, previews and cursor effects stay still.

    07 · Hard parts

    The tricky parts.

    • Keeping the music in sync. Browser timers drift, so the music is scheduled a little ahead on the audio clock. That's why every cut lands on a drum hit.
    • Lots of canvases. Thumbnails are drawn once and only animate while you're previewing. The big preview pauses when you scroll past it.
    • The cursor kept disappearing. Pop-up windows sit on their own layer above everything else, so the custom cursor moves itself back on top whenever one opens.

    08 · What's next

    What I'd do next.

    • Test it with real editors. Watch a few people find and cut a clip, and see how long it takes compared with the sites they use now.
    • Real clips and accounts. Hook up real video, and let people keep their collections and edits on any device.
    • A proper accessibility check. Go through everything with a screen reader and a keyboard, especially the timeline.
